Section 1

Read the product and user

Learning intention

Explain how purpose, user and context shape a product, and separate visible evidence from unsupported assumptions.

Success looks like
  • I can describe what a product needs to do before judging how it looks.
  • I can use observations from a reference image without treating the image as a construction plan.
  • I can connect form, function, user and context in a product-analysis statement.

A designer does not begin with decoration. A designer begins by asking what the product is for, who will use or view it, and where it will be used. These questions matter for a swinging sign, but they also work for a tea-light holder, a pot-plant holder or almost any designed product.

Start with purpose, user and context

Purpose is the job a product must perform. A sign may identify a place, communicate a name or message, guide a person, or help create a particular feeling. The user is not only the owner. The audience who reads the sign is also part of the user group. Context is the situation around the product: where it is viewed, how people approach it, what competes for attention and what conditions may affect its use.

A useful design question joins all three ideas: what must this product help this user do in this context? For example, a decorative sign for a quiet indoor space and a sign viewed briefly from farther away may need different visual priorities. That statement does not choose dimensions or a manufacturing method. It simply identifies the need clearly enough for design thinking to begin.

Read evidence before making claims

A reference photograph is evidence of appearance, not a complete plan. An observation names what can actually be seen: an oval panel hangs below a bracket; the bracket includes straight and curved forms; lettering and a cut-out image communicate a home theme. An inference is an explanation that goes beyond the visible evidence, such as naming an exact metal, joint, process, load or size. An inference may later prove correct, but it should not be presented as fact without an authorised source.

Careful designers label uncertainty. They can write ‘the photograph suggests’ when a visual clue supports an idea, and ‘local instructions will confirm’ when practical details are missing. This habit prevents a design folio from turning guesses into specifications. It also helps a team notice what information must be obtained before making begins.

Connect function and form

Function describes what a product does. Form describes its visible and physical character, including shape, line, proportion, colour, texture and arrangement. Form and function are related. A sign's lettering, negative spaces and overall silhouette affect whether its message can be recognised. A holder's shape affects how clearly it communicates where an item belongs. Curves can create a decorative character, while repeated forms can make separate parts appear related.

Good-looking form is not automatically successful. A highly detailed sign may suit a close-up display yet become difficult to read in another context. A plain form may communicate clearly but fail to express the intended character. Designers compare choices against purpose instead of asking only, ‘Do I like it?’

Build a useful product analysis

A strong product analysis has four moves: identify the purpose, name the user or audience, describe evidence from the product, and explain the likely effect of that evidence. A useful sentence pattern is: ‘The product uses ___ so that ___ can ___. This suits the context because ___.’ The explanation is more valuable than a list of features.

Compare more than one product when possible. Look for similarities, differences and trade-offs. One design might give priority to a bold message; another might give priority to decoration. Neither is automatically best. The better choice is the one that responds more convincingly to its own brief.

  • Describe visible evidence precisely.
  • Explain how the evidence affects purpose or audience.
  • Mark unknown practical details for local confirmation.
  • Avoid copying a reference product as though it were the only solution.

Key vocabulary

purpose
the job or need a product is intended to address
user
a person who owns, handles, views or is affected by a product
context
the setting and conditions in which a product is used or viewed
form
the visible and physical character of a product
inference
a conclusion drawn from evidence but not directly observed
Common misconception

If a detail appears in a photograph, the photograph tells us exactly how to make it.

A photograph can show appearance and relationships, but it does not confirm hidden materials, dimensions, joints, loads, tools or processes.

Takeaway: Read the need first, use visible evidence carefully, and judge form by how well it supports function for a particular user and context.

Local workshop boundary: Before any practical action, use only the plan, demonstration, risk controls and permissions supplied by your teacher. This section teaches design analysis, not workshop procedure.

No-stream learning pathway

Photo-reading routine

Distinguish observation from inference while identifying purpose, audience and context.

  1. Study a supplied product image for thirty seconds without writing.
  2. Record three details you can see and begin each with ‘I observe…’.
  3. Record two possible explanations and begin each with ‘This might mean…’.
  4. Write one question that must be answered before the image could guide practical work.

Evidence prompt: Write one product-analysis sentence that links a visible feature to a user need without claiming an unknown construction fact.

Section 2

Criteria, constraints and project pathways

Learning intention

Turn a need into a clear design brief, distinguish criteria from constraints, and compare project pathways without assuming missing specifications.

Success looks like
  • I can write a brief that names a user, need and intended result.
  • I can sort a statement as a criterion, constraint or question for confirmation.
  • I can compare pathways using the same design factors rather than treating one project as automatically easier or better.

A design problem becomes manageable when it is framed clearly. The brief gives direction, criteria describe what success should look like, and constraints identify the boundaries. Together they help students explore ideas without pretending that every local project detail is already known.

Write a brief that gives direction

A design brief is a short statement of the situation, user, need and intended response. It should be open enough to allow different ideas but focused enough to guide decisions. ‘Make something from metal’ is too broad because it does not explain who needs it or why. ‘Design a product’ is also weak because almost any outcome could satisfy it.

A useful structure is: ‘Design and produce a ___ for ___ that ___.’ The blank spaces name a product type, user or audience, and purpose. A pathway such as a Swinging Sign can be named when authorised, while exact size, material, hardware and process remain outside the brief until local information confirms them.

Separate criteria from constraints

Criteria are the qualities used to judge success. A criterion might ask whether the message is legible, whether the visual style suits the intended setting, whether the final idea can be explained, or whether waste has been considered. Strong criteria are specific enough to test with evidence. ‘Looks good’ is vague; ‘the intended viewer can identify the message in a teacher-approved viewing check’ is more useful.

Constraints are boundaries that limit the solution. Time, available resources, approved processes, the intended location and teacher-issued project requirements can all become constraints. A constraint is not automatically a problem. It can focus creativity by ruling out unsuitable directions. Unknown details should be recorded as questions for confirmation, not invented as constraints.

Understand the three project pathways

This course is centred on the Swinging Sign. The Tea-light holder and Scrolled pot-plant holder are teacher-directed alternate or additional pathways. The photographs establish the appearance of reference products, but they do not establish plans, sizes, materials, joints, loads, construction methods or assessment conditions.

The pathways can still be compared conceptually. Each requires a clear purpose, a user and context, communicated ideas, responsible material thinking, planned evidence and evaluation. Their specialised requirements may differ: a sign communicates a message; a tea-light holder relates to holding and presenting an LED light source; a pot-plant holder relates to containing or supporting another object. The approved plan and local instructions decide what students actually make.

Make priorities and trade-offs visible

Design criteria can compete. More decoration may create stronger character but reduce visual clarity. A larger number of separate features may communicate a detailed theme but make planning and checking more complex. A finish chosen for appearance may need to be reconsidered if it does not suit the intended context. A trade-off is the balanced decision made when improving one quality affects another.

Designers rank priorities rather than pretending every criterion is equal. They explain which criterion matters most for this user and purpose, which compromises are acceptable, and what evidence will be used to judge the result. This reasoning prepares an idea for teacher review without locking in any unconfirmed practical specification.

  • Need: what situation should improve?
  • Brief: what is being designed, for whom and why?
  • Criteria: how will success be judged?
  • Constraints: which confirmed boundaries must be respected?
  • Questions: which missing local details need teacher confirmation?

Key vocabulary

design brief
a focused statement of the user, need and intended design response
criterion
a quality or measure used to judge success
constraint
a confirmed boundary that limits possible solutions
pathway
an authorised project direction within the course
trade-off
a balanced decision in which improving one quality may affect another
Common misconception

A constraint stops creativity, so the best design has no constraints.

Every real design has boundaries. Clear constraints focus creative thinking and help rule out unsuitable ideas.

Takeaway: A strong brief names the need; criteria define success; constraints define confirmed boundaries; trade-offs explain why one response suits the chosen pathway.

Local workshop boundary: Your teacher confirms the project pathway, approved plan, materials, equipment, practical processes and any formal assessment conditions. Do not turn a photograph or course example into a specification.

Section 3

Workshop readiness and sustainability

Learning intention

Explain readiness as a thinking process and connect sustainability decisions across a product's life cycle.

Success looks like
  • I can distinguish a hazard from risk without inventing local controls.
  • I can explain why teacher instruction, authorisation and checking come before action.
  • I can compare sustainability choices across sourcing, production, use and end of life.

Workshop readiness is more than remembering protective equipment. It is the habit of pausing, understanding the task, recognising what could cause harm, following the current local controls, and checking that permission and conditions are in place. Sustainability also begins before making, because early design choices influence resources, waste, durability and what happens later.

Use the language of hazard, risk and control

A hazard is something with the potential to cause harm. Risk describes the chance and possible seriousness of that harm in a particular situation. A control is an action or condition used to reduce risk. These words are connected but not interchangeable. The same hazard can create different levels of risk when the task, people, environment or controls change.

Students should not invent controls from memory or from a general website. The current teacher demonstration, school procedures, equipment instructions and task-specific information determine what applies locally. A safety symbol or item of protective equipment is not a complete control plan by itself. Safe behaviour includes asking when uncertain and stopping when a condition differs from the instruction.

Complete a readiness check before action

A readiness check is a decision point, not a workshop procedure. It asks: Do I understand the goal? Do I have the approved plan? Has the teacher explained the task and current controls? Am I authorised to begin? Is the work area in the expected condition? Do I know what evidence I must record? If any answer is unclear, the correct next step is to pause and ask.

Readiness also includes personal responsibility. A student needs to be able to concentrate, communicate and respond to instructions. Rushing to catch up, copying another group's actions or assuming that yesterday's setup still applies can weaken safe decision-making. Calm preparation protects people, materials and time.

Think across the product life cycle

Life-cycle thinking considers a product from material sourcing through production, use, maintenance and end of life. A material may be recyclable, but that single fact does not make every design sustainable. Designers also consider how much material is needed, how efficiently it is used, whether the product is likely to last, whether parts can be maintained, and how choices affect people and environments.

For a metalwork project, responsible questions include whether an idea can meet its purpose with fewer unnecessary features, whether layout can reduce avoidable waste, whether a durable finish is appropriate to the intended context, and whether usable offcuts can be managed through the teacher's local system. The approved material and process still come from local instructions.

Record responsible decisions

Sustainability decisions need evidence. A folio can record an initial concern, the options considered, the choice made and the expected effect. ‘My design is sustainable because it uses metal’ is too simple. A stronger explanation might show how the design avoids unnecessary material, supports a useful life, and leaves confirmed production decisions to the approved plan.

Responsible design also includes social and ethical thinking. Products affect users, makers and communities. A designer should avoid unsafe assumptions, respect approved sources and images, and communicate honestly about what has and has not been tested. Sustainability is therefore a pattern of informed choices, not a sticker added at the end.

  • Pause when the task, plan, control or permission is unclear.
  • Use material efficiently without weakening the intended function.
  • Plan for a useful life and appropriate maintenance.
  • Record evidence for decisions rather than making broad claims.

Key vocabulary

hazard
something with the potential to cause harm
risk
the chance and possible seriousness of harm in a particular situation
control
an action or condition used to reduce risk
life cycle
the stages from sourcing and production through use and end of life
sustainability
meeting needs while considering environmental, social and economic effects over time
Common misconception

A product is sustainable as long as its material can be recycled.

Recyclability is one consideration. Material quantity, waste, durability, maintenance, production effects and end-of-life choices also matter.

Takeaway: Readiness means understanding, authorisation and checking before action; sustainability means tracing responsible choices across the product's whole life cycle.

Local workshop boundary: This section does not replace the school's risk controls or a teacher demonstration. Follow the current local instruction for every space, material, tool, technology and process.

No-stream learning pathway

Ready or pause?

Recognise when a learner has enough confirmed information to proceed and when they must stop and ask.

  1. Read four short planning scenarios supplied by the teacher.
